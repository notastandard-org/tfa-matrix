Documenting what is happening to you

Important: Always consider your physical safety first. Documenting abuse can help later — with police, lawyers, support services, or your own clarity — but only if doing it is safe. If the person causing harm might see you collecting evidence, stop and speak to a specialist service first.

If you need personalised support, contact 1800RESPECT (1800 737 732) or your local specialist domestic violence service. If you are in immediate danger, call 000.

Why document

Patterns of technology-facilitated abuse are often harder to prove than one-off incidents, because each individual message, notification, or login can look innocent in isolation. A clear record of what happened, when, and how often is what makes a pattern visible — to you, to the people supporting you, and to the systems that might intervene.

What to capture

  • The content itself — messages, posts, emails, screenshots of calendar invites, unexpected login notifications, account change confirmations.
  • Date and time — visible in the screenshot if possible, not cropped out.
  • Where it happened — which platform, which account, which device.
  • Who was involved — handles, phone numbers, email addresses. The other person may use accounts that don't carry their real name.
  • Context — a short note to yourself explaining what happened just before and after. Your future self will not remember.

How to capture it safely

  • Preserve originals. Don't delete the original message, post, or email until you've saved a copy somewhere the other person cannot reach. Original metadata (timestamps, sender details) is more credible than a copy.
  • Take full screenshots. Include the URL bar, the account indicator, and the timestamp. A cropped screenshot can be dismissed as fabricated.
  • Use a device the other person cannot access. A trusted friend's phone, a library computer, or a workplace device (if your workplace policies allow). Assume any device or account the other person has ever had access to may be compromised.
  • Don't rely on the platform keeping the content. Posts get deleted, accounts get closed, messages expire. Capture quickly.
  • Record your own narrative separately. Keep a simple dated log — a notebook, a private document, a secure notes app — describing what happened in your own words. Your recollection is evidence too.

Where to store it

  • Somewhere the other person cannot access. That rules out shared accounts, shared devices, shared cloud storage, and anywhere they have your password or recovery access.
  • Somewhere you can access later. A secure email address they don't know about, a password manager, a trusted person holding a copy, or a specialist service's secure intake.
  • Back it up. A single copy on a single device is a single point of failure. A second copy — printed, emailed to a trusted address, stored with a support worker — is worth the effort.

What not to do

  • Don't confront the other person with the evidence. It tells them what you know and puts you at risk. Evidence is most useful when they don't know you have it.
  • Don't edit the files. Cropping, annotating, or re-saving can reduce their credibility if they're later used in a legal process. Keep the raw originals; make annotations on copies.
  • Don't document at the expense of your safety. If the other person monitors your screen, your notifications, or your cloud backups, documenting can be dangerous. Get advice first.

When to involve a professional

If you are considering police, court, or a protection order, speak to a specialist domestic and family violence service or a lawyer before relying on evidence you've collected yourself. They can tell you what will carry weight in your jurisdiction, what procedural rules apply, and what other records (phone carrier data, platform subpoena responses) may be available to them that are not available to you.

For technical evidence specifically — suspected stalkerware, compromised accounts, unauthorised access — a digital forensic examiner can preserve evidence in a way that survives legal scrutiny. Removing suspected monitoring software before it is examined can destroy the evidence and tip off the person who installed it.

If you are supporting someone else

Hold the evidence for them if they ask you to. Knowing that a trusted person has a copy — separate from their own devices, accounts, and home — can be one of the most protective things you can offer.
